The phrase that stuck with me from this story was not technical. Australia's prime minister described an AI agent that "found a way around those blocks". In other words, an agent was told no by a system, and it kept going. Every company deploying agents, including in marketing, should study that sentence.

## What happened

At a [press conference in New York on 24 September](https://www.pm.gov.au/media/press-conference-new-york), Prime Minister Anthony Albanese said an OpenAI agent, used by the company's research team for internet research, gained unauthorised access to the Medicare Statistics Reporting Portal administered by Services Australia. When blocked, the agent looked for other routes. According to the PM, it accessed public and non-public information and, according to Services Australia, wrote files to an internal server, which is still being investigated.

The PM said the incident began on 18 June, and that OpenAI first notified the government on 10 September by email to a public mailbox, which he called unacceptable. He said there was no evidence that any individuals had been affected and no suggestion of foreign actors. The government set up a taskforce to review how existing processes handle AI-related cyber incidents and said it would seek advice on whether offences occurred.

[The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/openai-agent-medicare-statistics-portal-australia) reported that an OpenAI research group had set the agent to examine public spending on medicines, and that OpenAI told ABC News its models took actions it did not intend while looking up facts about Australia. The PM said he spoke with Sam Altman, who accepted that the company had not done well enough.

## Why it matters

Nobody in this story set out to break into a government system. A research agent was given a goal, met a barrier, and treated the barrier as a problem to solve. That is what goal-directed agents are designed to do. It is also exactly the behaviour that turns into an incident when the agent's goal is not paired with clear limits.

Marketing is full of agents with goals. Collect competitor prices. Monitor mentions. Scrape public reviews. Fill in partner forms. Test landing pages. Each of these involves interacting with systems you do not own, and each has its own "no": a login wall, a robots file, a rate limit, terms of service. An agent that politely stops at "no" is a tool. An agent that routes around "no" is a liability with your company's name on it.

The disclosure timeline is the second lesson. Months passed between the incident and the notification, and the government heard about it through a public inbox. If your agent causes a problem on someone else's system, how quickly would you know, and who would you tell?

## What my research says

Data collection across many sources is the foundation of my research. From [bBridge](https://doi.org/10.1145/2964284.2973836), our big data platform for social multimedia analytics (ACM Multimedia 2016), to our profiling work on [harvesting multiple sources](https://doi.org/10.1145/2671188.2749381), the craft was always about gathering signal responsibly and at scale. The responsible part is not decoration. I spoke about it at WSDM 2023 in [Under the Hood of Social Media Advertising](https://doi.org/10.1145/3539597.3575791), on the ethics and privacy of automated profiling and ad targeting.

Agents raise the stakes because they improvise. A traditional crawler does what its code says. An agent decides how to reach its goal. That means the constraints have to be part of the goal itself: what not to touch, when to stop, and when to ask a human. Any agent that acts or publishes automatically, including the kind we describe in our SIGIR 2026 paper on [agentic AI for professional visibility](https://doi.org/10.1145/3805712.3808371), needs explicit boundaries like these. They are what keep an agent useful rather than dangerous.

## A scope checklist for marketing agents

- Define the allowed territory. List the sites, APIs and accounts an agent may use. Everything else is out of bounds by default.
- Treat every block as a stop sign. Instruct and configure agents to halt and report on authentication walls, access errors, CAPTCHAs or rate limits, never to work around them.
- Respect terms and robots rules. If a source does not allow automated access, find a licensed data provider instead.
- Log every action. You should be able to reconstruct where an agent went and what it did.
- Set an incident path. Decide who is notified, and how fast, if an agent touches something it should not have. Notify affected parties directly, not through a public inbox.

Agents are becoming the way marketing work gets done. The companies that trust them most will be the ones that taught them when to stop.

## FAQ

? What happened with the OpenAI agent and Australia's Medicare portal?
According to Australia's prime minister, an OpenAI agent used for internet research gained unauthorised access to the Medicare Statistics Reporting Portal on 18 June after finding a way around blocks, and OpenAI notified the government on 10 September. He said there was no evidence individuals were affected and investigations were continuing.

? Why is this incident relevant to marketing teams?
Marketing increasingly uses agents to collect data, monitor competitors and interact with external sites. The incident shows that goal-driven agents may try to work around access barriers unless they are explicitly scoped and configured to stop.

? How can companies keep AI agents within safe limits?
Define allowed sites and accounts, make agents stop and report at any access barrier, respect terms of service and robots rules, log every action, and set a clear incident-notification process.
